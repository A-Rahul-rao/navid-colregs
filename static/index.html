∑ Mathematical Kinematics & Collision Geometry

Rigorous mathematical derivations implemented in colregs_math.js to compute CPA, TCPA, Relative Velocity, and Bow Crossing Range.

1. Local Tangent Plane Projection

Converts spherical geodetic coordinates (Lat, Lon) to Cartesian planar tangent coordinates (dx, dy) in Nautical Miles (NM):

dy = (Lat_target - Lat_own) * 60.0
dx = (Lon_target - Lon_own) * 60.0 * cos(Lat_mean)
Range = sqrt(dx^2 + dy^2)
Bearing = atan2(dx, dy) [deg]
2. Relative Motion Vector Decomposition

Decomposes SOG and COG into planar velocity vectors to solve relative closing speed:

V_own = [SOG_own * sin(COG), SOG_own * cos(COG)]
V_tgt = [SOG_tgt * sin(COG), SOG_tgt * cos(COG)]
V_rel = V_tgt - V_own = [v_rx, v_ry]
|V_rel| = sqrt(v_rx^2 + v_ry^2)
3. CPA and TCPA Analytical Derivation

Encounter distance squared over time t is D^2(t) = |P + t * V_rel|^2. Setting d/dt[D^2(t)] = 0 solves minimum distance:

TCPA = - (dx * v_rx + dy * v_ry) / |V_rel|^2 [hours]
P_cpa = [dx + TCPA * v_rx, dy + TCPA * v_ry]
CPA = sqrt(x_cpa^2 + y_cpa^2) [Nautical Miles]
4. Bow Crossing Range (BCR) Intersect

Determines the distance ahead or astern where the target track intersects own ship's heading ray:

t_bow = (dy * sin(HDG) - dx * cos(HDG)) / (v_tx * cos(HDG) - v_ty * sin(HDG))
BCR = Distance(Intersection) - SOG_own * t_bow

LEVEL 1 • THE NAUTICAL VOCABULARY & ACRONYMS
Every Term, Acronym, and Measurement Decoded
Own Ship vs. Target
Own Ship: The ship you are on and commanding (in our console: M/V PACIFIC PIONEER).
Target (or Contact): Any other ship, boat, or floating object detected by radar or radio in your vicinity.
Port vs. Starboard
Why don't sailors say "left" and "right"? Because if you turn around to face the back of the ship, your left becomes the ship's right! To prevent fatal confusion:
PORT: The left side of the vessel when facing forward (marked with a RED light).
STARBOARD: The right side of the vessel when facing forward (marked with a GREEN light).
Bow, Stern & Abaft the Beam
Bow: The pointy front of the ship.
Stern: The flat back/tail of the ship.
Beam: The widest point across the middle of the ship.
Abaft the Beam: Any direction behind the middle of the ship (an angle greater than 90° from the bow).
NM & Knots (Nautical Measurements)
NM (Nautical Mile): Equal to exactly 1,852 meters (1.15 regular miles). It is not an arbitrary number: 1 NM equals exactly 1 minute of arc (1/60th of a degree) along Earth's latitude lines!
Knots (kts): Speed measured in Nautical Miles per hour (1 knot = 1 NM/h ≈ 1.852 km/h).
HDG vs. COG (Heading vs. Course)
HDG (Heading): The direction the ship's physical nose is pointing (measured by the gyrocompass).
COG (Course Over Ground): The actual direction the ship is sliding over the seafloor (measured by GPS). Ocean currents and wind cause the ship to "crab" sideways, so HDG and COG are often slightly different!
SOG vs. STW (Speeds)
SOG (Speed Over Ground): True satellite GPS speed relative to the Earth's surface.
STW (Speed Through Water): Speed relative to the surrounding water flowing past the hull (measured by the Doppler speed log).
AIS & MMSI (Digital Ship Identity)
AIS (Automatic Identification System): An autonomous VHF radio transponder mandated on all commercial ships. It continuously broadcasts the ship's name, GPS position, course, speed, and size.
MMSI (Maritime Mobile Service Identity): A unique 9-digit digital telephone number assigned to each ship's radio in the world.
ARPA & VDR (Radar & Black Box)
ARPA (Automatic Radar Plotting Aid): The computer software on a ship's radar that tracks blips, calculates vectors, and predicts whether ships will collide.
VDR (Voyage Data Recorder): The maritime equivalent of an airplane's black box. It records all sensor data, engine commands, and radar plots for maritime safety audits.
LEVEL 2 • THE RULES OF THE ROAD AT SEA (COLREGS 1972)
The International Laws That Prevent Ships From Crashing

In 1972, the United Nations maritime branch—the IMO (International Maritime Organization)—created the COLREGS (International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea). Every captain and autonomous boat in the world must obey these rules.

The Two Core Roles: Give-Way vs. Stand-On

In any encounter between two ships where a risk of collision exists, international law designates one ship as GIVE-WAY and the other as STAND-ON:

GIVE-WAY VESSEL: Must take early, clear, and substantial action (altering course or slowing down) to stay completely out of the other ship's path. It is strictly forbidden from crossing ahead!
STAND-ON VESSEL: Has the right-of-way, but with a catch: it MUST maintain her course and speed! Why? So the Give-Way ship knows exactly what to expect and can steer around safely without second-guessing.
Rule 14: Head-On
The Hallway Rule: When two powerboats meet nose-to-nose on opposite courses, both ships are Give-Way! Both must turn to their RIGHT (Starboard) so they pass each other on their left sides (Red-to-Red).
Rule 15: Crossing
The Intersection Rule: If another ship is crossing from your RIGHT (Starboard), YOU must give way! Alter course to starboard to pass behind her tail. If she is crossing from your left, you are Stand-On.
Rule 13: Overtaking
The Slower Friend Rule: Any ship coming up on another ship from behind (more than 22.5° abaft her beam) is overtaking. The faster ship coming from behind must keep completely clear!
LEVEL 3 • SECRET SHIP HEADLIGHTS & OPTICS
How Ship Lights Work Like Floating Traffic Lights

Ships don't have headlights like cars that shine forward onto the road. Instead, under SOLAS Chapter V (Safety of Life at Sea), ships shine special colored lights in specific pie-shaped arcs:

Port Sidelight (RED 112.5°)
Shines from dead ahead to 22.5° behind the beam on the ship's left side. If you see a ship's RED light at night: RED MEANS STOP / GIVE-WAY! That ship is on your right and has the right-of-way.
Starboard Sidelight (GREEN 112.5°)
Shines from dead ahead to 22.5° behind the beam on the ship's right side. If you see a ship's GREEN light at night: GREEN MEANS GO / STAND-ON! You have the right-of-way; maintain course and speed.

Target Aspect Angle (α): This is the angle from the other ship's bow pointing toward you. Our algorithm calculates this angle to instantly know which light is shining toward our bridge, allowing the computer to make legal right-of-way decisions without human intervention.

LEVEL 4 • THE MATHEMATICS OF NOT CRASHING
CPA, TCPA, Relative Velocity & Bow Crossing Range

How does the computer look into the future? It uses geometric vector algebra:

CPA (Closest Point of Approach)
The Question: "If both ships hold their current course and speed, what is the absolute closest distance our hulls will get?"
If CPA = 0 NM, the ships will collide! Our system enforces a safety threshold of CPA ≥ 2.0 Nautical Miles. Any target with CPA < 2.0 NM turns bright red as a critical threat.
TCPA (Time to Closest Point of Approach)
The Question: "How many minutes from right now will that closest pass occur?"
It is an urgent countdown timer. If TCPA = 12 minutes, the bridge crew has 12 minutes to evaluate, communicate, and steer clear before the danger point.
Relative Velocity Vector (V_rel)
When you drive a car on a highway, oncoming cars seem to fly toward you at 120 mph because both speeds add together. Relative velocity vector (V_rel = V_target - V_own) subtracts your own movement so the radar can calculate how fast the other ship is closing in relative to you.
BCR (Bow Crossing Range)
The Question: "Will the other ship pass in front of my nose (ahead) or behind my tail (astern)?"
A positive BCR (e.g. +3.5 NM) means they cross ahead. A negative BCR (e.g. -2.1 NM) means they pass safely behind our stern. COLREGS forbids crossing ahead when giving way!

What is ARPA C2?
Stage 1 — What is Radar and ARPA?
Imagine you are in a foggy field and you spin around shouting "ECHO!" — the sound bounces off nearby trees and comes back to you. That is exactly how radar works, but with radio waves instead of sound. Ships send out invisible radio pulses; when those pulses hit another ship, they bounce back. The radar measures how long the round-trip took, so it knows exactly how far away that ship is.

ARPA (Automatic Radar Plotting Aid) is the smart computer attached to the radar that tracks automatically. Instead of a human drawing lines on a paper chart every few minutes, ARPA watches every blip on the radar screen and silently calculates: "That ship is moving at 14 knots, heading 048°T, and will pass 1.2 miles ahead in 9 minutes."
Stage 2 — What Does C2 Add?
C2 stands for Command and Control — a naval term for the full chain of detecting a situation, making a decision, and issuing orders to fix it.

An ARPA system alone only observes and reports: "Target 3 has CPA 0.4 NM in 7 min." A human must then decide what to do.

ARPA C2 goes one step further: it adds an autonomous decision engine that evaluates all COLREGS rules, computes the legally correct maneuver, and can directly command the vessel's helm (autopilot) or display the precise order on the bridge. It closes the full Observe → Orient → Decide → Act (OODA) loop without requiring a fatigued human in the chain.
Stage 3 — Technical Standards
ARPA displays are mandated by IMO Resolution MSC.192(79) and must meet presentation standards defined by IEC 62288. Requirements include:
• Target symbols: Unfilled diamond for tracked AIS targets; triangle outline for activated dangerous targets (CPA alarm).
• True motion vectors displayed as straight lines from target centers proportional to predicted future position at a selectable time (typically 3–12 min).
• CPA/TCPA alarm zones: User-configurable guard rings (default 2.0 NM / 15.0 min on most commercial systems).
• The C2 extension adds IEC 62940 (Integrated Bridge Systems) interconnects, feeding helm-order outputs via NMEA 0183 proprietary sentences or CAN-bus (NMEA 2000 / IEC 61162-3) to the vessel's ECDIS and autopilot.
What is the meaning of NMEA?
Stage 1 — A Universal Language for Instruments
Imagine all the instruments on a ship's bridge — the GPS, the speed log, the depth sounder, the compass, the radar — are like people from different countries speaking different languages. They each know something important, but they cannot talk to each other! NMEA was created to give every instrument on a ship a single common language so that any device can read messages from any other device.
Stage 2 — The Organization and Standard
NMEA stands for National Marine Electronics Association — a US trade organization founded in 1957 that writes the technical communication standards for all marine electronic equipment.

NMEA 0183 is a serial protocol running at 4800 bits per second over RS-422 twisted-pair wires. Every message is a plain-text "sentence" starting with $, a 5-letter talker and sentence identifier, comma-separated data fields, and a 2-digit hex checksum after *.

Examples used in this system:
$GPRMC — GPS Recommended Minimum (position, speed, course, date)
$HEHDT — Heading True from gyrocompass
!AIVDM — AIS radio message received over VHF
$SDDPT — Depth from echo sounder

What is a GNSS Receiver?
Stage 1 — Satellites Playing Hot-and-Cold
You know the game "Hot and Cold" — where someone tells you if you're getting closer to a hidden object? Satellites play that game with your phone or ship, except instead of saying "hot" or "cold," each satellite tells you exactly how far away it is. If four satellites all tell you their distance at once, the ship's computer can triangulate — do the math — and pinpoint exactly where on Earth you are, to within a few meters.
Stage 2 — GNSS Constellations
GNSS stands for Global Navigation Satellite System — the umbrella term for all satellite positioning networks. GPS is just one of several constellations:

• GPS (USA) — 31 operational satellites
• GLONASS (Russia) — 24 satellites
• Galileo (European Union) — 30 satellites
• BeiDou (China) — 35 satellites

A modern marine GNSS receiver can receive signals from multiple constellations simultaneously, tracking 14+ satellites at once. More satellites = better geometry = more precise position fix.
Stage 3 — RTK, HDOP, and Differential Corrections
Standard GNSS gives accuracy of about 3–5 meters. For precise vessel navigation, the system uses:

RTK (Real-Time Kinematic): A ground-based reference station whose exact position is known broadcasts correction signals. The ship's receiver computes the difference between the satellite signal and the known ground truth, correcting atmospheric delays in real-time — achieving accuracy down to 2 cm.

HDOP (Horizontal Dilution of Precision): A unitless quality factor describing the geometric spread of satellites across the sky. HDOP = 0.75 (displayed in our Sensor tab) means the satellite geometry is excellent. HDOP > 2.0 is poor; HDOP < 1.0 is ideal.

SBAS/WAAS: Space-Based/Wide Area Augmentation System — a network of ground stations that uplinks ionospheric correction data to geostationary satellites, which broadcast it to all GNSS receivers in range, providing 1–3 m accuracy without a local base station.
In-depth: Mathematical Kinematics & Collision Geometry
Stage 1 — What Does "Kinematics" Mean?
Kinematics is the study of motion — where things are, how fast they move, and where they will be in the future — without worrying about why they are moving. For ships, kinematics answers: "If Ship A is here going that way, and Ship B is there going another way, will they ever be in the same place at the same time?" That is the entire science of collision prediction.
Stage 2 — Vectors, CPA, and TCPA Visualized
Every ship has a position (where it is on the map) and a velocity vector (an arrow showing direction and speed). The computer draws these vectors forward in time and asks: do these paths ever get dangerously close?
Both ships travel in straight lines (vectors). The closest they ever get is marked by the orange circle — the CPA (Closest Point of Approach). The time from "now" to that moment is the TCPA (Time to CPA).
Stage 3 — Relative Motion Diagram
Easier method: freeze Own Ship and let the target move with the relative velocity (V_rel = V_target − V_own). The closest the target ever gets to the frozen own ship is the CPA.
The CPA is the perpendicular distance from the fixed own-ship to the target's relative-motion track line. If that distance is less than 2.0 NM, the system raises a collision alarm.
Stage 4 — The Exact Mathematics
Step 1 — Convert Lat/Lon to flat-plane Cartesian NM (Local Tangent Plane approximation, valid <50 NM):
dx = (Lon_target − Lon_own) × 60 × cos(Lat_mean) [NM East] dy = (Lat_target − Lat_own) × 60 [NM North] Range = √(dx² + dy²) [NM]
Step 2 — Decompose velocities into East/North components:
V_own = (SOG_own × sin(COG_own), SOG_own × cos(COG_own)) V_tgt = (SOG_tgt × sin(COG_tgt), SOG_tgt × cos(COG_tgt)) V_rel = V_tgt − V_own = (v_rx, v_ry)
Step 3 — Minimize future squared distance and solve for TCPA:
D²(t) = (dx + t·v_rx)² + (dy + t·v_ry)² Differentiate, set dD²/dt = 0: TCPA = −(dx·v_rx + dy·v_ry) / (v_rx² + v_ry²) [hours] If TCPA > 0 (vessels still converging): P_cpa = (dx + TCPA·v_rx, dy + TCPA·v_ry) CPA = √(x_cpa² + y_cpa²) [NM] If TCPA ≤ 0 → vessels already diverging.
What is IEC 62288 — and what is IEC?
Stage 1 — What is a Technical Standard?
Imagine you are building a traffic light. Without any rules, one city might make green mean "stop" and red mean "go" — total chaos! Standards are agreed-upon rules that say: "Green ALWAYS means go, red ALWAYS means stop, worldwide, no exceptions." Technical organizations write these rules so that engineers in Japan and engineers in Germany can build equipment that works perfectly together.
Stage 2 — What is IEC?
IEC stands for the International Electrotechnical Commission — a non-profit global standards organization founded in 1906, headquartered in Geneva, Switzerland. It is the world's leading authority on standards for electrical, electronic, and related technologies. Over 170 countries are members.

IEC works alongside ISO (International Organization for Standardization): ISO handles mechanical, chemical, and business standards; IEC handles all electrical and electronic standards. For marine electronics specifically, IEC publishes the entire IEC 62000-series of maritime navigation and radio-communication equipment standards.
Stage 3 — IEC 62288 Specifically
IEC 62288: "Maritime Navigation and Radiocommunication Equipment and Systems — Presentation of Navigation-Related Information on Shipborne Navigational Displays — General Requirements, Methods of Testing and Required Test Results."

In plain language: this standard defines every detail of how information must be visually displayed on a ship's bridge screens — radar, ECDIS, ARPA — so that an officer of the watch on any ship in the world sees the same standardized symbology, colors, and layout:

• Colors: Dangerous/activated targets = triangle outline (crimson). Safe targets = diamond outline. Own ship = filled vessel shape.
• Vectors: True motion vectors as straight lines from symbol centers, length proportional to predicted speed over configurable time (3–12 min).
• Alarms: CPA/TCPA alarm zones must be user-configurable, visually distinct, and audible.
• Display Modes: Head-Up (vessel heading pointing to screen top), North-Up (chart North pointing up), True Motion (own ship moves across fixed chart).

This app's color palette — cyan for own ship, emerald for safe targets, crimson for dangerous targets, amber for caution — is derived directly from IEC 62288 presentation guidelines.
What is WGS (and WGS-84)?
Stage 1 — Earth Is Not a Perfect Sphere
Here is a surprising fact: Earth is not a perfect round ball — it is slightly squashed at the poles and bulging at the equator, like a spinning top. This means if you try to draw a flat map of the Earth, you need to decide what exact "shape of the Earth" you are using as your starting model. If two people use slightly different models, their GPS coordinates will not match — a ship's GPS says one position, a paper chart shows another. Disaster!
Stage 2 — WGS Defined
WGS stands for World Geodetic System — a standardized mathematical model of Earth's shape and gravitational field created by the US National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency (NGA).

WGS-84 (the 1984 edition, still in use today) defines Earth as a reference ellipsoid with these precise parameters:
• Semi-major axis (equatorial radius): a = 6,378,137.0 m
• Semi-minor axis (polar radius): b = 6,356,752.314 m
• Flattening: f = (a−b)/a = 1/298.257223563

This means Earth is 21.4 km wider at the equator than from pole to pole.
Stage 3 — Why It Matters for Navigation
Every GPS satellite broadcasts its position in WGS-84 coordinates. Every GNSS receiver on every ship, airplane, car, and phone on Earth computes its position in WGS-84. Every digital nautical chart (ECDIS S-57/S-101 format) is georeferenced to WGS-84. This universal adoption means a position from a Japanese GPS chip matches perfectly with a German chart plotter.

In this application's kinematics engine, positions (Lat, Lon) are expressed in WGS-84 decimal degrees. The Local Tangent Plane projection converts them to flat Cartesian NM using the WGS-84 ellipsoid's local radius at the mean latitude, ensuring geometrically accurate CPA/TCPA results even at high latitudes where longitude degrees shrink in physical length:
dx = ΔLon × 60 × cos(Lat_mean_WGS84) [NM East/West] dy = ΔLat × 60 [NM North/South] cos(Lat_mean) corrects for WGS-84 ellipsoid's converging meridians toward the poles.
